Monday

Horses, Hot Springs and a Pigeon

  • Orvieto
  • San Casciano dei Bagni

6 photos

Scroll

Monday began with horses. At half past nine we were at an equestrian club near Orvieto, in a place called La Cacciata, and for two hours we rode around the surrounding countryside. The guide spoke Italian, the horses, it seems, did too, but everyone came to an understanding. We rode and liked it very much. There's nothing more to add — if it's good, it's good.

By noon our next question had ripened: where are the warm pools? We want to swim. The answer was found in San Casciano dei Bagni. This is real Tuscany now, not that far from Orvieto, a little hilltop town that lives on hot water. There are dozens of springs there; the Etruscans and Romans bathed in them. And a few years ago archaeologists pulled a whole collection of bronze statues out of an ancient sacred pool — they had lain in the hot mud for more than two thousand years.

Around half past one we had lunch at the restaurant Daniela on the square. A Tuscan menu, no compromises: pecorino with truffle, pici — a thick hand-rolled pasta, pappardelle with wild boar. All of this is what we were advised to order. Tuscany knows how to feed you so that after lunch you don't much feel like swimming any more, but you very much feel like lying down.

But we came to swim, after all. We went to look for the famous free bathing pool. I find a stone basin under a canopy, there's water, the stone is old, everything is genuine. I take a photo and send it: “Is this it?” Turns out — it isn't. It's an old washhouse, a lavatoio, where the local women washed laundry for centuries. Good thing I asked before I climbed in. Picture it: an American priest in swimming trunks sitting in a medieval washhouse, waiting for it to get warm.

In the end we swam at Fonteverde, a paid thermal pool. The water is about thirty-eight degrees. In November, getting into water like that is the right decision; there's nothing even to argue about.

In the evening we returned to Orvieto and, you won't believe it, couldn't find the cathedral. In a town where the cathedral can be seen from the plain several kilometers away, we walked the little streets and couldn't understand where it had gone. We found it. And around half past seven we stood in front of the facade under its night lighting.

This is, without exaggeration, one of the most beautiful facades in Italy. The cathedral was built over almost three hundred years, and the first stone was laid on November 13, 1290 — which makes it almost exactly seven hundred thirty-six years ago, this week. The chief master of the facade was the Sienese Lorenzo Maitani. Below are four marble pillars, and they are the Bible in stone: on the left the creation of the world, on the right the Last Judgment. Above is the rose window by the Florentine Orcagna and gold mosaics that blaze under the lights. And the central bronze doors are quite new, from the sixties, the work of Emilio Greco; on them are the works of mercy. The town argued about them for some ten years, and now the cathedral is unimaginable without them.

And this whole cathedral was built for the sake of one linen cloth from Bolsena, where we were yesterday. For the sake of one doubt that the Lord did not leave unanswered. We stood, listened to the story about the cathedral and looked up.

We had dinner at the trattoria del Moro Aronne. I had pigeon alla ghiotta — it's an Umbrian dish, and I kept checking: is it really pigeon, not partridge? Pigeon. Well, after yesterday's donkey, nothing surprises me any more.
