Viterbo: The Papal Hill in the Rain

  • Tuscania
  • Viterbo

13 photos

Scroll

It was raining in Viterbo. Not a downpour, but that Italian November drizzle that makes the cobblestones shine as if varnished and the yellow leaves stick to the stones. We climbed up to the Colle del Duomo, the cathedral hill. This is the oldest place in the city: according to tradition, first there was an Etruscan sanctuary here, then a fortress, and in the thirteenth century the popes came.

Why would the popes want Viterbo, you may ask? Rome was dangerous at the time. Noble families fought right in the streets, and a mob could drive out even the pope himself. Viterbo, on the other hand, was loyal, rich and well fortified. For almost a quarter of a century the popes lived here, and from those years the loggia remains, from which they blessed the people: seven pointed arches on slender paired columns, stone lace, with the sky showing through it.

This is also where the word “conclave” was born. In twelve sixty-eight Pope Clement the Fourth died, and the cardinals sat down to elect a new one. They sat for a year. Then a second. The townspeople got fed up and locked the cardinals in a hall with a key, cum clave. It didn't help. Then they were put on bread and water. It didn't help. Then the roof was taken off the hall. In the thirty-third month a pope was finally elected, and what's more, a man who was not a cardinal and was in fact in the Holy Land at the time. And the new pope later wrote down a rule: from now on, lock them in straight away.

I went around with my own home-made tour: I photographed the map and the signs, and the story played in my earphones. In the museum I asked for the rest to be sent as text. Reading is faster than listening, and in a museum there is never enough time.

The cathedral museum is small, but it has everything at once. A golden Gothic folding altarpiece: the Mother of God enthroned, with Peter, the Forerunner, John the Theologian and Paul on either side. Essentially our Deesis, only gathered into a single frame. A small Madonna on a gold ground; the Child has coral beads around His neck and a goldfinch in His hand. Relics of St Lawrence, the archdeacon to whom the cathedral here is dedicated: a bone in a wooden casket with glass and a handwritten label. And a Roman Artemis of Ephesus, the very one about whom, in Acts, they shouted for two hours: “Great is Diana of the Ephesians!” Now she stands by the wall of the papal palace, hung all over with her strange clusters, and nobody shouts in front of her.

Two things I examined longer than the rest, for professional reasons. The first: a red chasuble, embroidered all over with gold and silver, with a coat of arms at the bottom. I stood in front of the case counting stitches the way someone else might count money. The second: “The Dead Christ”, a painting from eighteen seventy-six. The body on a slab, a white shroud, the removed crown of thorns on the floor. This is our epitaphios, only painted in oil. We stitch it and carry it out on Great Friday, and here it hangs in a museum gallery, and next to it you want to fall silent just the same.

In the evening we returned to Tuscania and went to dinner at the same place as on the first evening. We ordered Chianti. On the label was a beast very much like the Etruscan Chimera of Arezzo. We are, of course, still not in Tuscany, but the Etruscans caught up with us even here.

Tomorrow is our last day with the car. We want to make it to Cerveteri, to the Etruscan burial mounds, and we need to be at Fiumicino by one in the afternoon.
