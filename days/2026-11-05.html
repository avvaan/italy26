Pompeii and the Road Up

  • Pompeii
  • Agerola

21 photos

Scroll

Pompeii. It seems everyone knows about it: Vesuvius, the year 79, a city under ash. We covered that at school. But here's what is usually forgotten: the city we walked through today is much older than Rome as we know it. It was founded by the Oscans, some six hundred years before the Nativity of Christ. Then the Greeks lived nearby, then the Samnites came, and only for the last century and a half of its life did Pompeii live as an ordinary Roman town.

Ordinary — that, perhaps, is the whole point. Pompeii is not a capital and not a holy place. It's a provincial town with streets, shops, baths and taverns, which one day simply stopped. In Rome you look at the great ruins of a great empire. Here you look at someone's house, someone's bakery, someone's street with cart ruts and stepping stones across the roadway so you don't get your feet wet in the rain. Two thousand years have passed, and everything is arranged very understandably.

The eruption was described in detail by Pliny the Younger; he saw it from the other side of the bay. His uncle, Pliny the Elder, commanded the fleet, sailed off to rescue people and died. Excavation of the city began only in the eighteenth century, and it's still being excavated. In the nineteenth century archaeologists thought of filling with plaster the hollows left in the ash by people — that's how the famous casts came about, which still make you uneasy today. And above all this, as if nothing had happened, stands Vesuvius. It, by the way, hasn't gone anywhere and hasn't gone to sleep: it last erupted in 1944, within our grandfathers' memory.

And one more thought, this time from a priest. Less than twenty years before the eruption, very close by, in Puteoli — that's today's Pozzuoli, on the other side of Naples — the Apostle Paul came ashore when he was being taken to Rome. In Acts it says just that: “Where we found brethren.” That is, there were already Christians on this bay. Who knows, maybe some of them walked these very streets too. It can't be proved, but it's hard not to think about it.

Friends, the advice is simple: come to Pompeii with time to spare and in comfortable shoes. The city is large, the pavements are made of real Roman stone, and you could never call them even. And it's good that we're here in November and not in August: Pompeii is one of the most visited places in Italy, and under the summer sun on these stones it's probably not pleasant at all.

And towards evening we went up to Agerola, high above Amalfi. The road up is a serpentine, bend after bend. Agerola is a few villages on a mountain plateau, about six hundred meters above the sea. From here, by the way, begins the famous Path of the Gods, which runs above the coast. We decided to stay here, up top, for two nights. Why not down below, by the sea, where everyone is? Because here there's somewhere to park the car. And I don't drive into town centers any more. The lesson of Naples has been learned.

Originally, to be honest, Pompeii and Agerola were supposed to happen a day earlier. But we stayed longer in Naples, and the plan simply shifted. Nothing terrible: Pompeii has waited almost two thousand years, it waited one more day.

Tomorrow we'll go down to the sea.
