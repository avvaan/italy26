Tarquinia: Necropolis, Museum and the Sea

  • Tuscania
  • Tarquinia

29 photos

Scroll

Today was the day of the Etruscans. From Tuscania to Tarquinia is twenty-five minutes, and all the way we listened to a story about the city of the dead, which for two and a half thousand years has looked across the valley at the city of the living. Right by the necropolis, in a yard under an olive tree, we saw a block of stone: a bearded man reclining as if at a feast, around him owls, horses, animal faces. At first we thought it was ancient. It turned out to be modern work by a local craftsman; nearby stand a van and plastic chairs. But it's done with real knowledge.

From the outside, the Monterozzi necropolis doesn't look at all like a city of the dead: a green meadow, cypresses, and in the grass little houses, like sheds. You open the door, and there's a steep staircase down into the tuff. Below there's glass, you press a button, the light comes on, and out of the darkness step people who were painted five hundred years before the Nativity of Christ. They feast, dance, play double flutes.

I came up with my own tour. I photographed the sign at the entrance, and a minute later in my earphones there was already an account of what to look for on the left, on the right, above the door. So we went from tomb to tomb: the Leopards, the Lionesses, the Bacchantes, the Hunter, where the whole room is painted like a hunting tent, and through the painted fabric a roe deer grazes. In the Tomb of Hunting and Fishing there are two rooms and one door, and that very youth who dives from a cliff into the sea can't be seen from the first room. We had to look at him in a photo on the phone. You stand a meter away from him and look at your phone.

And you can see very clearly how the Etruscans changed. In the early tombs there's only feasting, dolphins, birds and joy. But two hundred years later, when Rome was taking their cities from them one after another, demons with hammers already stand at the painted door to the afterlife, and the dead are led by the arms. In the Tomb of the Two Roofs the deceased is seen off by a she-demon with a torch, and at the gates of Hades he is met by his own, who died earlier. To my mind, this is the hope most understandable to us of all that we saw.

Then there was a separate story with lunch. The navigator led us straight through a ZTL, and we didn't want to risk a fine, so we parked the car by the Barriera San Giusto gate and went on foot. The restaurant I had picked turned out to be locked up. The second one too. November, Wednesday, Italy. The third one was found, with the local acquacotta soup on the menu and with a note under the carbonara saying that the one who loves you is not the one who talks about love, but the one who silently cooks you carbonara.

After lunch we went to the museum, in Palazzo Vitelleschi. It was built in the fifteenth century by a cardinal who led the papal armies and ended his life in Castel Sant'Angelo. Inside is everything that was taken out of the ground. An urn in the shape of a hut; through its little window the ashes of a warrior can still be seen. A bronze helmet with a crest like a flame. The tomb of the Versna family with a chariot, spears and dishes for an eternal feast. And, of course, the Winged Horses. They were found in '38 as a heap of fragments and put together like a jigsaw puzzle. After a whole day underground it was like a breath of air: light, wings and movement upwards.

And upstairs, in the medieval halls, there was a fresco of the Mother of God. Beneath it in Latin: “When you come to the image of the Most Pure Virgin, as you pass by, do not forget to say: Hail.”

Towards evening we drove to the sea, to the old salt pans and to Lido di Tarquinia. We just wanted to sit with a hot tea and look at the cold sea. Somewhere beyond the salt ponds, they say, flamingos spend the winter.
