Sunday. For Catholics it's the feast of All Saints, and also the first Sunday of the month, when museums let you in for free. We didn't go to the Liturgy this time — we hadn't prepared and hadn't brought anything for the service with us. The priest is on vacation; that happens too. We gave the whole day to ancient Rome.

We approached the Colosseum and immediately understood: free admission is wonderful, but not for us. The line was such that we looked at it from outside. Never mind, it has been standing there for almost two thousand years, it can wait.

We walked along the Sacred Way, the Via Sacra, past the columns of the Temple of Venus and Roma. A design by Emperor Hadrian himself. According to tradition, the architect Apollodorus, who laughed at this design, was later executed. Criticism was a risky business in ancient Rome.

On the ruins of this temple stands the church of Santa Francesca Romana. There is the tomb of St Frances of Rome — a wife, a mother, she served the poor, and beside her is her guardian angel. An icon of the Mother of God from the twelfth century, of the “Sweet Kissing” type, and under it they found an even more ancient one, an encaustic one, from the fifth or sixth century. And a stone on which, according to tradition, the Apostle Peter knelt when the demons lifted Simon Magus into the air. That's what it says, in Italian. There is also a medieval tombstone of a prelate, on which you can see the whole “pattern” of the vestments: a bell-shaped chasuble, an amice, pontifical gloves. For me, as a priest, this is especially interesting to look at. We stood for a little while at the feast-day Mass — there was almost nobody there.

Nearby are the excavations of Vespasian's Temple of Peace, where the menorah and the table of the shewbread from the Temple in Jerusalem were once kept. And behind them is Santi Cosma e Damiano, the first church on the Forum, the year 527. A sixth-century mosaic: Christ descends on fiery clouds, Peter and Paul lead the holy physicians to Him, below are twelve lambs. And it is precisely today, the first of November, that is the feast of the Unmercenaries Cosmas and Damian. You can't arrange a coincidence like that. On the mosaic there is an inscription with the word salus — in Latin it means both health and salvation. For unmercenary physicians you couldn't put it more precisely.

There is also a tombstone: an eighty-six-year-old father buried his son of forty-four. And the inscription — “so overturned is the order of nature.” The Latin is short, and there's nothing more to say.

Then the Imperial Forums: of Augustus, of Nerva, of Trajan. On the Forum of Nerva there's a frieze with weavers and Minerva — remember the myth of Arachne? And on top of Trajan's Column now stands the Apostle Peter. The emperor would probably have been surprised.

At Trajan's Markets we walked along a real Roman street, the Via Biberatica, past shops with travertine frames. There are stamps on the bricks; on one roof tile, the stamp of King Theodoric and a chi-rho, the monogram of Christ. There's also a colossal head, said to be of Trajan's mother. From the terrace you can see all the forums at once.

We had lunch at a café by the forums. Then the bronze Aeneas carrying his father Anchises on his shoulders at Palazzo Valentini, and at the end we went up the Vittoriano, to the Altar of the Fatherland and the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. There, up top, we decided: next we head south — Naples, Pompeii, Amalfi, Matera, Bari to the relics of St Nicholas. Getting ahead of myself, I'll say: life will rework this plan too.

Tomorrow we leave Rome.
