The morning began with parking the car, but I've already written about that. Then breakfast from the hotel, which in fact turned out to be a croissant with coffee at the nearest café around the corner. The croissant, by the way, was tasty, and so was the coffee. So no complaints.

Then on foot, about twenty minutes, to St Peter's Square. You can't put into words how monumental those colonnades are. You really feel like a little speck of fluff. In everything you sense the greatness of the Church that built this, and the smallness of a cog in this huge mechanism. We had no guide. Yana and Masha took pictures of themselves against everything, and I looked and tried to fit at least something into my head. You can't see it all or take it all in. You touch not even the summit, but just the edge of a great culture.

We went into the shops. Almost everywhere they have the Kazan icon of the Mother of God. And October 31, by the way, is the feast of the Apostle Luke, who, according to tradition, painted the first icons of the Mother of God. A nice echo.

The line at the basilica is enormous; you could spend a whole day there. We hesitated for a long time whether to stand in it or not. But we went in. The basilica astonishes both with its size and its beauty. The main dome is about forty meters in diameter. And the small dome at the very top, above it, is almost the same as the dome of the church in Tishki. Only in Tishki it seems big, and here, up top, it's tiny.

In the bookshop there was a separate shelf in Slavonic and Russian, and editions from '62 at that, just from the time of the Second Vatican Council. I bought “Guidance for the Celebration of Orthodox Divine Services.” Interesting, isn't it? They've kept it to this day and sell it in the Vatican shop.

Below, under the basilica, are the tombs of the popes. Including those we talk about at our Friday talks. On one grave it is written that the most important thing in this man's life was that he “promoted reconciliation with the Eastern Church.” And the tomb of Alexander VII, the one who built the colonnade — Death under a red stone shroud, and Truth covered by a bronze veil the color of white marble. You can't look at it enough.

A sea of tourists, from every country. The staff strictly make sure nobody sits down anywhere even for a minute. I asked one of them why they chase people away. He, irritated: they block the whole staircase. Also true.

However much you'd like to think you imagined it — no. The cult of the Pope really does exist. In the bookshop almost everything is about him; the letters two meters high at the base of the dome are about him. A little unusual. On the other hand, throughout the basilica there are statues of the founders of orders. A good idea — to single out those who started each direction of ministry. In this, it seems to me, lies part of the secret of the Western Church's success in mission and social work.

We had lunch at a small restaurant we would never have found on our own. We chose the local Roman pasta. To me, macaroni is macaroni, like in Orlando, no better.

In the evening, Castel Sant'Angelo — built as the mausoleum of Emperor Hadrian, it later became a papal residence and fortress. Right by the entrance is the Pope's hall (the Pope again); on the walls little figures are doing all sorts of things. Probably a medieval Instagram feed. I can just picture it: a rocking chair, a dog, a fireplace, and over a year the Pope goes through all the walls, then starts again. And at the end of the day — a sunset with the basilica in the background.

Only half of today's plan happened, and that's fine.

Tomorrow — ancient Rome.
