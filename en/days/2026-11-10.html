In the morning we went to Orvieto Cathedral again, inside this time. It was raining. Inside, the walls are striped — black basalt and light travertine, as if the church had been drawn in pencil. In the Chapel of the Corporal is kept that very cloth from Bolsena. Two days ago we stood where the blood fell on it, and now we stood before it itself. The circle closed. Opposite is the Chapel of San Brizio, where Luca Signorelli painted the Last Judgment. They say Michelangelo came here to learn before painting his own in the Sistine Chapel. Then we walked around the town following a list drawn up in advance.

We booked the accommodation for the last three nights only this morning. We live without a margin, but with parking by the house, and for me that's the main thing.

Around two we left Orvieto and all the way listened to a story about these places. Lake Bolsena is a collapsed volcano. On the small island of Martana in 535 the Gothic queen Amalasuntha was strangled, and that started a twenty-year war. And in Montefiascone there's a wine called “Est! Est!! Est!!!” A bishop's servant, who marked the taverns with good wine, wrote “it is” on the door here three times, and the bishop never went on to Rome.

We did stop in Montefiascone. The first thing I did was proudly park the car right under a sign “eccetto titolari permesso NRP,” that is, for locals only. We moved it. We went up to the Rocca dei Papi, the summer papal fortress. The view of the lake from there, they say, is the best. They say — because there was fog and rain. I asked about local news: during the day near Acquapendente, very close by, there had been a small earthquake, a 2.6.

From four to five we sat on the waterfront in Capodimonte and watched the sunset over the lake. Opposite is the island of Bisentina, nearby the castle of the Farnese — the very family that produced Pope Paul III.

In the evening we checked in in Tuscania. I asked: was D'Artagnan a Tuscan? No, a Gascon. And we, for the record, aren't in Tuscany at all. Tuscania is Lazio, the region of Tuscia, the old land of the Etruscans. We had dinner at the restaurant “Beer and Meat”: Morellino and Grechetto, Neapolitan starters — arancini, frittatina, cuoppo, then steaks and the “Rasna” board. Rasna is what the Etruscans called themselves. On a Tuesday evening Tuscania is empty. Nowhere to go, nobody around.

But Stefano turned up, the husband of our apartment's hostess. For fifty euros he drove us to the free hot springs of the Piscine Carletti near Viterbo and waited while we bathed. I translated through my phone, by voice, and we talked about everything. About how our months come from the Romans. I told him: when my ancestors lived in dugouts, yours were building temples, forums, Pompeii; a great country and a great history; it's a great honor to be here. Then about small Italian cars and American trucks. Gasoline in Italy is now two twenty to two fifty a liter, and it used to be a euro twenty. Stefano's car runs on gas. I told him that in Ukraine I also drove on gas, and in America gasoline was a dollar seventy-five a gallon, now four. And about the police: in America they chase you with flashing lights; in Ukraine they would stop you and offer a fine or a bribe. Stefano also learned how in San Casciano we nearly bathed in a washhouse.

In the evening there were only men at the springs. We asked why. Stefano: I don't know, generally both men and women come. He also recommended a restaurant for tomorrow, I Tulipani: they feed you well there, and fish too, tell them you're from Stefano, she knows me well.

Tomorrow, the Etruscans and the necropolis of Tarquinia. And for lunch, it seems, we'll go “from Stefano.”
